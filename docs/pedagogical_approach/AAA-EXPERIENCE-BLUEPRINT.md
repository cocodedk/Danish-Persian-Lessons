# AAA Experience Blueprint

## Experience promise

“I can make, hear, and recognize something Persian that matters to me.”

The first release should optimize for voluntary engagement and foundational Persian literacy, not
for covering all 32 letters quickly. The experience can become broad later; the opening must be small.

## Age is a design input, not profile decoration

The child journey cannot be truly AAA until it selects a primary developmental band.

| Likely band | Opening style | Reading load | Guide tone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5–8 | Narrated play, direct manipulation, short story | Almost none | Warm character companion |
| 9–12 | Puzzle, making, mystery, personal mission | Short, optional detail | Clever peer-like guide, never babyish |
| 13+ | Identity, creativity, real messages and media | Concise but richer | Respectful coach, minimal mascot behavior |

Until the age is known, prototype the interaction in an age-neutral way: one beautiful Persian item,
one clear action, strong sound, restrained praise, and no childish copy.

## Current and proposed routes

Current first run:

```text
Open app -> six-step explanation -> alphabet index -> 39-item course -> name -> words
```

Recommended child first run:

```text
Open child journey
  -> choose: my name / mystery word / tiny story
  -> watch and hear one Persian item
  -> trace or build one meaningful part
  -> retrieve once with gentle reveal
  -> keep a personal artifact
  -> choose: one more / done for now
```

Recommended return:

```text
Open -> one inviting continuation -> today's 2–5 minute mission
     -> collection and free exploration remain visible but secondary
```

The grown-up notebook keeps the current open lesson library, detailed progress, pronunciation help,
and review tools. Switching journeys must be deliberate and persistent, not a mode toggle a child can
hit accidentally.

## The first 90 seconds

### 0–10 seconds: invitation

- Show one vivid, inspectable Persian object: the learner's name, `آب`, or another chosen family word.
- Pair it with a real image or personal artifact, not a generic course dashboard.
- Give one large action such as “Make the word,” “Hear it,” or “Find the first shape.”
- Keep settings, IPA, counts, and the course map outside the primary visual hierarchy.

### 10–35 seconds: guided action

- Demonstrate right-to-left direction through the movement itself.
- Ask for one tap-to-place, trace, or matching action.
- Keep help visible; the first act is guided practice, not a test.
- Respond within one interaction frame with local visual and spoken feedback.

### 35–60 seconds: meaningful reveal

- Complete the whole item and read it aloud.
- Connect the Persian form to the image, Danish meaning, or learner's own name.
- Use teaching motion to show joining or a stroke, then stop.
- Let the learner replay sound without navigating away.

### 60–90 seconds: agency and closure

- Ask one recognition question with no timer.
- Reveal the full teaching card after either answer.
- Add the item to a visible personal collection.
- Offer “one more” and “done for now” with equal dignity.

## Candidate hooks to prototype

Test three independent openings. Do not ask the daughter to evaluate a menu of concepts in prose.
Let her use each one.

### A. My Persian name

Show the completed spelling early, then teach one recognizable body or dot pattern from it. The whole
name is a promise and artifact, not a test of letters that have not been taught.

Strength: identity, family connection, existing repository support.  
Risk: transliteration review, privacy feelings, and possible school-assignment tone.

### B. Mystery word

Start with a strong real image such as water, flower, book, or bread. Build and hear the short Persian
word, then reveal its Danish meaning.

Strength: immediate visual comprehension and existing lesson images.  
Risk: can feel like ordinary flashcards unless the action and reveal have character.

### C. Tiny message

Help a recurring guide complete a two- or three-word message to a family member. The first session may
teach only one word; the message grows over several visits.

Strength: purpose, story, and relatedness.  
Risk: higher content, illustration, audio, and continuity cost.

Recommendation: prototype A and B first. Let observed preference decide whether C becomes the larger
narrative wrapper.

## Learning loop for every new item

Use the existing normative sequence in child-sized form:

1. **Orient:** point attention to one new shape, sound, or direction.
2. **Model:** show, hear, and connect it to meaning.
3. **Notice:** contrast one useful near-neighbour.
4. **Guide:** trace, assemble, match, or read with help.
5. **Retrieve:** ask once without answer-defining help.
6. **Reveal:** show the complete explanation after every attempt.
7. **Revisit:** return after other items and on a later day.
8. **Transfer:** use it in a name, word, phrase, or message.

No session must include all eight stages. A session should end at a natural success and resume later.

## Child home information hierarchy

The first phone viewport should answer, in order:

1. What can I do now?
2. What will I make or discover?
3. How long will it take?
4. Can I choose something else or stop?

Below that viewport:

- My Persian things: names, words, messages, stickers, and writing samples.
- Explore: a small set of unlocked or open activities grouped by interest.
- Practice: due items expressed as a short mission, not debt.
- Grown-up notebook entry, visually quiet and protected from accidental activation.

Do not lead with streak, level, remaining alphabet count, settings, or exhaustive lessons. Those are
supporting systems, not the child's reason to begin.

## Interaction and audiovisual craft

- Use native human Persian audio for modeled items; never autoplay on page load.
- Motion must explain direction, joining, stroke order, or result. Respect reduced motion.
- Provide trace, copy, and freehand stages, with a non-drawing alternative for motor accessibility.
- Avoid harsh handwriting rejection. Give useful shape feedback and allow “show me” without penalty.
- Keep touch targets at least 44 by 44 CSS pixels for this touch-heavy experience.
- Use real lesson images and authored illustrations that reveal the item clearly.
- Keep the exercise-notebook material, but add controlled contrast and a recurring living presence.
- Avoid a one-note beige page: paper is a material, while teaching ink, images, artifacts, and controls
  provide the visual energy.
- No timers, lives, social ranking, autoplay loops, manipulative notifications, or reward scarcity.
- Sound-off, keyboard-only, screen-reader, and reduced-motion paths must complete the same lesson.

## Parent-child relationship

The app should support three positions without forcing one:

- **Independent:** the child can complete the full short activity alone.
- **Beside:** a parent can hear the same word, answer a cultural prompt, or celebrate the artifact.
- **Afterward:** the grown-up notebook suggests one optional real-world use, such as finding the word
  in a book or writing it together on paper.

Parent guidance should not appear as instructions over the child's shoulder. It belongs in the
grown-up journey. The child owns the play session and can decline the follow-up.

## Daughter-led prototype test

Use a three-minute, no-explanation test for each candidate hook.

1. Say: “I made a small Persian thing for you. You can try it or stop whenever you want.”
2. Put the device down and do not point at the first control.
3. Observe the first ten seconds, first tap, hesitations, requests for help, and whether she continues.
4. Do not rescue a confusing screen; confusion is the result.
5. Ask afterward: “What did you think it was?”, “What did you want to tap?”, and “What would make you
   open it another day?”
6. Let her change one thing directly: word, guide, sound, image, reward, or task.

Avoid “Did you like it?” and avoid defending the design. Her first glance is product evidence.

## Success measures

| Measure | First prototype target |
|---|---:|
| Understands the first action without adult explanation | Yes |
| Time to first voluntary action | 10 seconds or less |
| Time to first meaningful Persian result | 60 seconds or less |
| Requests help before first result | No more than once |
| Completes or consciously exits the session | 100% |
| Chooses “one more” | Useful signal, not a quota |
| Returns without a reminder within seven days | North-star behavioral signal |
| Recognizes at least one taught item the next day | Learning signal |

Do not add analytics to collect these. Record a tiny moderated observation note with her permission.

## First implementation risks

| Change | Risk class | Main risk | Containment |
|---|---|---|---|
| New child entry route | Routing and persistence | Existing orientation/profile redirects | Isolated route and explicit journey setting |
| Early name artifact | Curriculum and content | Testing untaught forms or showing a poor spelling | Model only; teach one reviewed part; preserve edit path |
| Touch writing | Component and accessibility | Motor frustration, canvas semantics, device variance | SVG/pointer prototype plus tap alternative and permissive feedback |
| Child/adult front doors | Information architecture | Duplicate content or confusing switching | Shared catalog/progress; separate shells; one persistent choice |
| Narration and guide | Asset and content | Childish tone, audio coverage, load cost | Prototype one age-neutral guide and three reviewed clips |
| Home simplification | Workflow contract | Hiding open browsing or review information | Change child hierarchy only; retain grown-up library |

## Acceptance for the first vertical slice

- The initial viewport contains one obvious, playable Persian action.
- No methodology, IPA, progress debt, or settings compete with that action.
- The learner can hear, see, and manipulate the same reviewed item.
- The first action teaches before it tests.
- Wrong, reveal, skip, and stop paths are warm and preserve all progress and rewards.
- The complete result and next choices remain visible on a 360 by 640 viewport.
- Every action has touch, keyboard, and assistive-technology semantics.
- Motion and sound are optional; the learning relationship remains understandable without either.
- The daughter can complete or leave the experience without parental operation.
- A moderated test records what she did, not what adults hoped she would do.
